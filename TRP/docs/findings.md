# Findings

What the install, the game's code and the recordings have shown, and the traps met on the way: moved out of `AGENTS.md` on 2026-10-10. Each entry is backed by a test, a probe check or a recording. Read the part that bears on what you are about to touch; the specs under `spec\` hold the behaviour itself.

## Reading the game's code

- The decompiler is ILSpy's command line, in `local\tools\ilspycmd` (ignored). It runs on the installed .NET 10 runtime: `dotnet local\tools\ilspycmd\pkg\tools\net10.0\any\ilspycmd.dll`.
- The whole of `Assembly-CSharp.dll` is decompiled to `local\decomp\src` (8,620 files, one per type, in namespace folders). To redo it: `ilspycmd -p --nested-directories -o local\decomp\src -r <Managed folder> <dll>`. It takes a few minutes.
- The SPT-patched assembly is fully readable: every type has a real name.
- Read with Grep and targeted Read calls; the big classes are thousands of lines (`EFT\Player.cs` is 28,000). Write what you learn into `docs\spec\` as you go, because a long reading session outlives the context it started in.
- Where to look: `EFT\MovementContext.cs`, `EFT\MovementState.cs` and the `*PlayerState.cs` files (the two prone transition states are `Transit2ProneState.cs` and `Prone2StandState.cs` at the top level), `EFT\SpeedLimiter.cs` (the hard caps by state), `HardwareTime.cs` and `EFT\GameWorld.cs` (the player's clock), `EFT\MoveInputTranslator.cs` (keys to commands), `PhysicalBase.cs` (weight and stamina), `EFTHardSettings.cs`, `EFT\GlobalConfiguration.cs` (the shape of `globals.json`), `AnimationSystem\RootMotionTable\`, `EFT\Animations\ProceduralWeaponAnimation.cs` and its neighbours, `EFT\PlayerAnimator.cs` (animator parameter names).


## What the data showed

These are established from the install and covered by tests or probe checks.

**Bundles.** UnityFS, serialized file format 22, type trees embedded in every bundle looked at. No external type database is needed. `Windows.json` maps each bundle key to its dependencies; the file on disk is `StreamingAssets\Windows\<key>`. The `shaders` (360 MB) and `cubemaps` bundles are dependencies of nearly everything and are loaded only on demand. Loading the AKM with all dependencies takes about 0.6 s.

**Weapons.** A weapon's bundle (`Prefab.path` in `items.json`) holds a `WeaponPrefab` script on the `*_container` object. Its `_weaponObject` is the animated root with the meshes. Under that root: both arm bone chains from the collarbones (`Base HumanLCollarbone/...`), `Camera_animated`, and `Weapon_root/Weapon_root_anim/weapon` with the `mod_*` slot bones, markers and moving parts. Meshes, materials and clips live in sibling bundles reached through references.

**Stock build.** `globals.json` `ItemPresets` has the default preset (`_encyclopedia` equals the weapon's template id). Each part names its parent item and a `slotId`, which is the name of the bone it attaches to on the parent's prefab.

**Part orientation.** Every prefab is stored upright with its geometry turned -90° about X underneath; slot bones live inside that turned space. A part therefore sits at its bone's origin with its root oriented like the weapon's root, not like the bone. Inferred from the data, not yet checked against the game's code.

**Detail levels.** `LODGroup` components list the renderers of each level. Only level 0 is kept.

**Arms.** A hands bundle holds one skinned mesh and a `Skin` script with `_bonePaths` from the body root. The suffix after `Base HumanRibcage/` matches the weapon prefab's arm bone paths. The app skins the arms to the weapon's arm bones directly; in the game they go through the body skeleton.

**Animation.** Clips are generic transform clips, not humanoid muscle clips. Tracks name bones by CRC-32 of the path below the animated root. Curves are stored streamed, then dense, then constant. These clips store rotation as Euler angles in degrees, applied X, then Y, then Z; that order reproduces the prefab's rest rotations to within 0.04° on all 81 rotation tracks of the AKM idle clip. `customType` is 0 on every binding.

**Animation sets.** `WeaponPrefab._originalAnimatorController` is a 7-layer controller with 128 clip slots (104 distinct clips for the AKM); its clips move the arms, fingers and weapon parts. `_animatorSimple` and `_animatorSpirit` are override controllers whose generated clips move only `Weapon_root`, `Weapon_root_anim`, `weapon` and `fireport`. `_fastAnimatorControllerBinaryData` is a baked copy of the controller in the game's own format; unread.

**Textures.** Weapon textures are DXT5 (`_diff`, `_nrm`) and DXT1 (`_gloss`). Normal maps keep X in alpha and Y in green. Gloss maps are greyscale. Rows are stored bottom-up, so raw upload with unflipped UVs is correct. Materials use a specular/gloss shader with `_MainTex`, `_BumpMap`, `_SpecMap`, `_Color`.

**Coordinates.** Unity to Bevy is a Z flip: positions `(x, y, -z)`, quaternions `(-x, -y, z, w)`, triangle winding swapped, tangent sign negated, matrices `S·M·S`. The simulation works in Unity's space with yaw positive to the right and pitch positive downward; the app negates both for Bevy.

**Movement data.** Movement is root motion from the body animator. The install ships it precomputed in `assets/content/characters/rootmotiontable/playerrootmotiontable.asset.bundle` (185 states, velocities per clip, blend trees over animator parameters); format and evaluation are in the movement spec. Freeform Cartesian blend weights computed with Unity's algorithm match the table's stored grid exactly, and are computed directly because the grid stops short of the local player's parameter range. The body animator controller is `assets/content/characters/controllers/player_anim_controller.bundle` and the local player prefab is `assets/content/commonprefabs/playersuperior.bundle`; neither is read yet.

**Engine settings.** `globalgamemanagers` (loose, no type trees) holds the physics step and gravity as the first fields of the time and physics manager objects: 1/60 s and -9.81. The weapon springs advance once per physics step with formulas that have no time step in them, so the rate is part of the behaviour. The movement simulation also runs at 60.

**Weapon motion data.** `ProceduralWeaponAnimation` and `PlayerSpring` sit on `Base HumanRibcage` of `playersuperior.bundle`, with the motion parameter sets and walk presets as separate objects in the same bundle; all have type trees. The procedural motion acts on `Weapon_root/Weapon_root_anim` in the weapon's bone space (x right, -y along the barrel, z up). The camera is not a child of `Camera_animated`: it rests at `CameraOffset` beside the weapon root and takes only that bone's rotation. The weapon's `Camera_animated` and the player prefab's `Camera_animated_3rd` each rest at -90° about X; the game multiplies them, blends from a half turn toward the product by the head-bobbing setting, and undoes the half turn, so at rest the camera is unturned. The stock AKM is 3.495 kg with 40 ergonomics.

**Weapon values override the prefab.** When a weapon is taken in hand its item template replaces values serialized on the player prefab: the turning points (`RotationCenter`, `RecoilCenter`), most of the rotation recoil spring, `CameraSnap`, and the position spring's damping (from `globals.json` `Aiming.RecoilHandDamping`). Read the template, not the prefab, for anything per-weapon.

**Animator states and marks.** A weapon's controller has a `Hands` layer with one state per action (58 for the AKM), each with a speed and a clip or a blend tree of variants. The game's timed marks are not Unity animation events: each state has an `AnimationEventsStateBehaviour` naming a list in an `AnimatorControllerStaticData` object beside the controller, with marks such as `FiringBullet`, `ShellEject`, `MagOut`, `MagIn`, `AddAmmoInChamber` and `Sound`, at fractions of the state. `Hands.FIRE` plays `akm_fire` at speed 10 (0.097 s). `probe states` prints them all.

**Player settings.** Defaults in the game's settings classes: field of view 50 (range 50 to 75, vertical degrees), head bobbing 0.2 (0.2 to 1), mouse sensitivity 0.7, aiming sensitivity 0.5. **This install's own are not the defaults:** SPT keeps the player's settings as JSON, one file per page of the game's menu, in `E:\spt\SPT_Runtime\user\sptSettings` (`Game.ini`: `FieldOfView` 74, `HeadBobbing` 1.0; `Control.ini`: `MouseSensitivity` 0.7, `MouseAimingSensitivity` 0.5), which is what every trace's header shows. The app starts with those since 2026-10-09 (`Install::player_settings`). The file of the same name under `%AppData%\Battlestate Games` belongs to the live game and says something else (head bobbing 0.2): not that one.

**Hands.** The weapon rig's arm chains are not what the game shows. It solves the body's arms onto `weapon_L_IK_marker` / `weapon_R_IK_marker` under the `weapon` bone, bends the elbows toward `Bend_Goal_Left` / `Bend_Goal_Right`, and replaces the left target with a `GripPose` object on a part (the stock AKM's handguard has two) while the clip keeps the hand on the weapon. `Hands.IDLE` plays `akm_idle_armed`; the `Hammer` layer plays the same clip on its three masked bones.

**The body carries the weapon.** The weapon's own controller has no sprint state. The player's skeleton has `Weapon_root_3rd_anim` under `Base HumanSpine3`, beside the ribcage; the body's clips move it and carry curves (`Weapon_3rd`, `First_Person_Curve_Weight`, `Hand_Left`, `Hand_Right`, `Shoulder_Left`, `Shoulder_Right`, `Position_cache`) that drive animator parameters of the same names. Each frame `PlayerBones.ShiftWeaponRoot` draws the first-person weapon bone toward that body bone by `Weapon_3rd * First_Person_Curve_Weight`, keeping the procedural motion as an offset. Both curves are 1 in the sprint clips. The weapon's rig is set each frame to the ribcage's position and the view's rotation (`MovementContext.DefaultRotationFunction`).

**The body animator.** `Player_anim_controller` is the controller the game asks for; the bundle also holds `..._CURRENT_REWORK_ACTUAL_USE_THIS`, a backup and a test. 22 layers. Its clips are generic, about 175 bone tracks, with 7 to 16 float curves each; a `spirit` set with 3 tracks and the same curves exists for a simplified player. A state has one blend tree per layer that plays it (`m_BlendTreeConstantIndexArray`, a typed integer array): the sprint's base tree is freeform Cartesian over (`Direct_X`, `SprintSpeed`), and the synchronised `Sync_SprintHands` layer picks the arms and weapon pose by `WeaponTypeFloat`, then `WeapSizeModifier` (the weapon's width in inventory cells: the stock AKM and M4A1 are 5, the PM pistol 2), then `ThirdPersonFloat`. A blend-tree leaf's `m_Duration` is what its clip's length is multiplied by. In a sprint the additive aiming layer switches itself off.

**The sprint zoom is dead code.** `ProceduralWeaponAnimation.Sprint` has a setter that would take 10 degrees off the field of view, and nothing assigns it. Check that something calls a setter before implementing what it does.

**Animator transitions.** Each has an exit time, a blend length (seconds if fixed, else a fraction of the state being left) and an offset into the next state, plus conditions on parameters. From the weapon's `IDLE` every action is entered with no blend; the reload chain blends for 0.33, 0.32 and 0.18 s and leaves its states at 0.30, 0.78 and 0.90 of their length. `probe states` and `probe bodystates` print them.

**Recorded scenarios run on real ground.** In the third recording scenarios 07 and 09 ended 3.2 m and 1.8 m off level. Heights from a trace are not a test of the flat-ground simulation.

**Frame time in the weapon animation.** `Player.VisualPass` gives the weapon animation the physics time elapsed on frames where a physics step ran and the frame's own time otherwise, and the camera code runs every frame with it. Above 60 frames a second the camera therefore runs ahead of the clock (1.5 times at 120). `Hands::frame` reproduces this; hosts call it once per rendered frame.

**Sway spring.** The settings object overrides the prefab: 8 rad/s and damping 0.8 for a sound character (`SWAY_FREQ_NORMAL_DAMAGED`, `SWAY_DAMPING_NORMAL_DAMAGED`), not the prefab's 7.

**Skills matter.** The recorded character aims a third faster than an untrained one and walks faster (Strength). Anything compared with a trace has to take such values from the trace; `replay` does. The app takes `--aim-speed-bonus`.

**Scripted turning.** The plugin's turn rates pass through the game's mouse sensitivity (0.70 for this user), so a scripted 120°/s is 84°/s in the game. The replay follows the recorded look direction instead.

**Springs.** The game's `Spring` draws `Current` to `Zero` and then reads `Zero + Current`, so a held zero counts twice at rest. Confirmed by trace: a full lean rolls the camera 10°.

**What the traces showed about movement (2026-10-07).** The user's character: Strength about 40 (top walking speed setting 0.686, sprint parameter limit 1.83), 7.5 kg carried by the inertia shown (0.052), no overweight, field of view 74, head bobbing 1.0, mouse sensitivity 0.70, camera near plane 0.03. Acted on so far: top speeds from Strength (`MovementSettings::set_strength`, `--strength`), the speed parameter starting at the selected speed and holding while standing, no sprint from a press made before moving, four footsteps per locomotion cycle. With those, the speed and sprint parameters tracked the game within 0.01 and 0.05 and the gaits within about 4%. The start of a walk, the reversal and the stop were then still different; the body's animator and the hard cap on speed (below) settled all three. The tables are in `docs\spec\movement.md`, "Compared with the running game".

**The body's animator (2026-10-07).** With no `client.config.json` in the install the local player's body runs Unity's own animator on `Player_anim_controller` (22 layers), not the game's fast animator. Scripts on its base-layer states name the movement state (`EPlayerState` numbers), and the movement state changes when the animator enters such a state, at the start of the blend into it. The movement code only raises flags (`Inert`, `Sprint`, `IsJumping`, `Landing`) and sets parameters. Clips carry root motion as curves bound to the animator (`customType` 8) and say per clip whether level and vertical travel go out as motion or stay in the pose (vertical stays, in every clip looked at). Clips also carry curves that drive parameters of the same name (`FootStep`, `Weapon_3rd`, `First_Person_Curve_Weight`, `Aiming_Layer` and others). The install's root motion table stores the engine's own weights for 76 two-parameter blend trees; the three blend functions in `rootmotion.rs` reproduce them (freeform directional only among its children; its angle weight is 1, not the 2 of the published description).

**The view hangs from the ribcage.** The weapon rig and camera are placed each frame at the body's ribcage bone with the view's rotation. The eye's height, its drop crouching, its shift in a lean and its bob all come from the body's clips; nothing about them is a figure of our own any more.

**The hard cap on speed.** The character controller allows each movement state a top speed set by Strength (standing 0.5 to 1.5 m/s, walking 3.6 to 4.6, sprinting and jumping 6.6 to 7.9) and cuts the movement to it after the fact. That is what stops a walk short: the recorded character drops from 2.94 to 1.17 m/s in one frame when the standing state begins. These figures are defaults written in the game's code, not in the install's data.

**Other rules the third recording confirmed.** A landing caps the selected speed for a while (0.456 for 0.43 s for the recorded character, from `Inertia.BaseJumpPenalty` and its neighbours). The selected speed is put back to the wheel's setting at every change of state. Aiming caps the speed setting at 0.33. The jump's push-off grows with Strength (`1 + 0.2 * level / 50`). The game moves the player once per drawn frame, and several of its rules work per frame, not per second.

**The player's clock.** In a raid the frame time the player's code gets is not the engine's: `GameWorld` reads a stopwatch once a frame in whole milliseconds (`HardwareTime`) and hands the difference to `Player.UpdateTick`. At 120 frames a second it runs 8, 8, 9 ms. The animator and root motion run on the engine's time. The game's velocity figure is the frame's motion over the stopwatch's step, so the motion over that figure gives the step back: exactly 8.0 or 9.0 ms on every recorded frame with motion in it. The hard cap on speed is checked against that figure, so it bites on the short frames and not the long one. `replay` derives the step per frame and passes both times.

**What the walking state keeps.** The game has one object per movement state for the player's life, and entering the walking state does not clear the rates of its two SmoothDamps, `TransitionCoef` or the last input seen. The direction's rate at the moment a walk ends (4.4 per second back along the walk, for the recorded character) sits there until the next walk and bends its start. On the frame the state is entered its input is the tiny starting direction, not the key. The sprint state is a second object with its own fields. In `movement.rs`, `enter_move`.

**The bearing matters.** Entering the walking state from a sprint, the game sets its speed vector to (0, 1) in world axes. Facing -z the vector has to cross zero to reach the walking target and the speed parameter falls faster than facing +z. `replay` runs on the recorded bearing for this reason; do not put its yaw back to zero.

**Prone.** The prone key only sets the animator's `Prone` flag; the animator does the rest through clips of its own (`idle_to_prone` 1.0 s, `prone_to_idle` 1.03 s, `Sprint_To_Prone` 1.0 s and 2.2 m), each naming a movement state (`Transit2Prone` 19, `ProneIdle` 2, `ProneMove` 3, `Prone2Stand` 18). The crawl blends eight clips by direction alone, so the speed wheel does nothing to it: 0.5 m/s forward, 0.4 back, 0.275 sideways, from the root motion table. Lying down the stance is forced to 0, so getting up always takes the animator's crouch variant. The hard caps by state are a table in `SpeedLimiter` (crawling 1.2 to 1.5 m/s; states not in it get the walking cap).

**Turns on the spot.** A clip can turn the root as well as move it: the turning is a quaternion in the engine's motion curves 10 to 13, beside the path in 7 to 9. Only the four standing turn clips use it (a quarter turn in their first 0.4 s). The animator enters them by itself when `DeltaRotation` passes 45 degrees; the code's `Turn` parameter does not exist in the controller. The sidestep is built since 2026-10-08. An earlier note here said it reached into the weapon's motion through a sidestep value: it does not. `SidestepFloat` is only an animator parameter, read by the layer that bends the body with the view, and `IdlePlayerInertia` only sets the playback speed of the step's clips.

**The ground is a ray's reach away.** A character counts as on the ground if a ray down from its capsule finds ground within a check distance: 0.07 m to begin with, 0.08 once it has walked or crawled, 0.15 once it has sprinted, 0.03 once it has jumped (set at the change of movement state, kept otherwise). Gravity only acts off the ground. So on ground that falls away a walking character rides up to 8 cm over it and a sprinting one 15, and only a jump or a fall brings it down. A recorded standing jump lands 8.0 cm below where the character stood; that, not the simulation, was the "late landing". `replay` puts level ground at the height a recorded jump lands on. Heights in a trace are measured from wherever the character stood after the settle, not from the ground.

**Speed limits.** A fall of 0.15 m or more caps the selected speed like a landing does, by curves of the height (`Inertia.SpeedLimitAfterFall...`, `SpeedLimitDuration...` in `globals.json`), in the landing's slot: lower limit and later end win. A running jump's cap therefore lasts 0.82 s, not the landing's 0.43. A changed limit takes effect at the next once-a-frame look, which is after the ground check and before the animator.

**The body's angle to the view is a frame old.** The game works it out only when it turns the body, inside a state's update, after the animator has run. The standing follow, `DeltaRotation` and `Aim_angle` all see last frame's. `Movement::note_rotation` is that moment; hosts that set yaw or body yaw directly call it.

**A state update can run twice.** The animator calls on the movement state once for each of its states that names it and had a part in the frame (current, being entered, left this frame). Standing and the turn on the spot both name the standing state, so on the frames they share the standing update runs twice. `Body::calls`.

**Where in a frame a transition starts.** An animator transition waiting for an exit time starts at that point within the frame, and the rest of the frame goes to the blend and the next state; one started by a condition starts with the frame over, its next state at its offset. The same holds for the weapon's animator: a state started by a command has run none of its time when the frame ends. Recorded to the frame for both.

**The root's turning comes off the pose.** In a turn clip the pelvis track turns with the character and the motion curves carry the same turn out as root motion; the pose has to be turned back by it, as the root's travel is taken off. Without that the eye swung 20 cm round the body in a turn.

**The weapon's logic runs per drawn frame.** Trigger, shots, reload stages: all in the frame's update, after that frame's physics steps; the springs feel a shot on their next step. `replay` steps `Firearm` once per frame, and since 2026-10-08 so does the app.

**Weight changes within a recording.** A reload can drop the old magazine, and the header's `inertia` then differs from one scenario to the next (0.052, 0.050, 0.047 in the fourth). `replay` fits the carried weight per scenario. The recorded round count jumps to the new magazine's at the start of a reload.

**Start settled.** A body made fresh is not one that has stood a while: some layers rest in short clips that take 0.13 s to reach the pose they hold, and the spring that lags the body's height behind the feet takes seconds to reach its resting stiffness. `Body::new` and `Movement::new` start both settled; `replay` also takes the idle's phase from the trace.

**The weapon goes to the body's hand.** Besides `Weapon_3rd`, the body's clips carry `Hand_Right` and `Hand_Left` (and curves for the shoulders and elbows). Each, times `First_Person_Curve_Weight`, takes that arm from the weapon: the arm's solver gets the rest as its weight, and while the right hand's share is below 1 the game moves the weapon so that its right-hand marker lies on the body's right palm (`PlayerBones.Kinematics`, after the hand-over to the body's weapon bone and after the arms are solved). The crawl's clips have every one of these at 1; the sprint's have the hands at 0 for a rifle. That, not the turn-away, was the 22.8 degrees and 9 cm by which the weapon in a crawl was off. `Hands::weapon_transform_carried` takes the palm (`Movement::held`) and the marker (from the host's rig, or `trp_sim::right_hand_marker`).

**The turn-away is off in a crawl.** Entering the crawl clears a flag in the weapon motion's mask and the overlap depth is then zeroed every step. Before suspecting an effector, check the mask.

**A curve missing from a clip counts as the parameter's default.** Blending on the first layer between a clip that carries a curve and one that does not, the engine takes the default (0 for all of the body's) for the one without. Lying still has no `Hand_Right` curve, so into a crawl the parameter rises with the blend.

**A lying body is laid to the ground, with a fault.** Going down, lying and crawling the game turns the body's animated part to point along the ground (from the point under it to the point half a metre ahead) with the ground's normal as its up, both eased at 5 per second, and then limits the roll to between -8 and 8 degrees using an angle the engine reports from 0 to 360. Any roll to the right beyond 0.0057 degrees therefore becomes 8 degrees to the left. The animator sits on that part, so root motion comes out turned too: a crawl to the right with the body rolled climbs. Getting up undoes it from 0.3 of the clip on, with no limit, so the false roll goes in one frame. On the recordings' ground (0.22 degrees) the fourth recording, facing one way, has no roll and the fifth, facing the other, the full 8. `World::ground_normal` is what a host gives for it.

**First-person poses.** A body animator state can name an `AnimatorPose` (offsets for the weapon and camera, eased in while the state is the one last entered). Only the rolls lying down have one. They are added to the *rest* of the weapon's springs, and a rest counts twice once the spring has caught up: the pose's 35 degrees turn the weapon 70. The pose's camera position is dead code (it hangs on a field nothing sets); its camera rotation is live. The speed they come in at is `Inertia.WeaponFlipSpeed` at the character's inertia, not the prefab's 1. Built: `Hands::assign_pose`, `Hands::blend_poses`.

**The game follows its own frame rate.** Its springs step 60 times a second and nothing is drawn between the steps (in a 120 fps trace `hands_rot` and the weapon bone change every other frame). Its camera code runs every frame on a time that is the physics time on frames with a physics step and the frame's own otherwise, so above 60 fps the aiming camera, the camera's follow of the muzzle and the field of view's easing are faster and uneven (the camera's steps alternate about 2 to 1 at 120 fps). Its movement rules work per frame. Its weapon logic runs per frame. None of this is ours to imitate with the host's frames: the simulation gets frames from `Pacer`.

**What takes the aim away.** `NO_AIM_STATES` in the settings object (a list of bytes, read by `HardSettings::bytes`): 3 crawling, 5 sprinting, 6 jumping, 7 falling, 9 breaching a door, 10 looting, 11 picking up. The firearm controller drops the aim on entering one and refuses it in one; nothing restores it. A reload drops it below mastering level 2 and then ignores the aim key both ways; from 2 it keeps it and runs the "tactical reload" blend. The body's animator has a flag `isAiming`: its use is read and built (below, "The body leans into the sights"). In the user's game `mastering` is 0, so the first reload case is the one it shows.

**The sprint is a flag before it is a state.** `Physical.Sprinting` (the stamina object is paying for a sprint) comes on when the sprint key is accepted in a walk forward. The animator's sprint state follows when the speed has wound up, 0.23 s later for the recorded character. Whenever what the stamina pays for changes, the firearm controller is told the flag, and told it is on it lowers the sights and lets go of the trigger. `Movement::sprinting` is that flag; the recorder writes it as `sprinting`.

**Only an idle weapon lowers its sights when told.** The three calls that lower them from outside (a no-aim state entered, the sprint on, a jump or fall) go to the firearm's operation in hand, and only the idling one (and a bolt action's shot) acts; the others inherit a method that only logs. Check what the operation in hand does with a call before assuming the controller's method name says it all.

**The camera is worked out before the body takes the weapon.** `FirstPersonStrategy` places the weapon bone by the procedural motion, then the camera from the sight on it; the hand-over to the body's weapon bone and the body's palm comes later in the frame (`PlayerBones`). An aiming camera fed the carried weapon is off by however far the body swings it: 22 cm getting up from lying.

**The body leans into the sights.** Flag `isAiming` on the body's animator, set by the firearm controller whenever its aim changes; layer `Additive_ISaim` (the tenth) answers with clips by stance, or by weapon type lying down. 14 mm at the ribcage. Before suspecting the camera for a gap that appears with the sights, look at `ribcage_p`.

**The game's state scripts say more than the state.** Each `PlayerStateContainer` on an animator state gives the movement state, which part of a movement it is (`Type`: none, in, out), a side (`AdditionalDirectionInfo`), a length, and whether it wants a state object of its own (`CreateUniqueMovementStateObject`: only the sprint does, which is why the sprint is "a second object"). The part and side are handed over at every entry; the length only when the objects are made, so states sharing an object share whichever length came last. `probe bodystates` prints them (`<movement 15 in right length 0.333 s>`).

**A transition with no blend still gets the rest of the frame** when it is taken at an exit point. Recorded on the sidestep's way in.

**Lying still, the step key waits a frame.** The lying state only remembers the step, sets the lean to 0, and gives the step to the animator at the end of its own update, after the animator has run. Standing it goes at once.

**The recorder sees the frame as drawn.** It writes in `Camera.onPreCull`, when the game has turned the ribcage to the view and scaled it (1, 1, s) for a wide field of view. Everything below the ribcage (the arms) is recorded squeezed along the view; the body's own weapon bone, which hangs from the spine, is not. `ribcage_q` in a trace is the view, not the bone's animated turning.

**Only the left hand's fingers go with the hand.** The left finger poser's weight is the left hand's figure each frame; the right one's is set to 1 when the weapon is taken and left there.

**In this install the step's playback never accelerates.** `TiltAcceleration` is 0 to 0 in `globals.json` (the code's default is 4.5 to 1.5), so `SidebackSpeed` sits at `TiltStartSideBackSpeed` for the inertia.

**A reload ends for the logic before its animation.** `ReloadExternalMagOperation` hands over to the idling operation at `MagIn` when nothing is left to chamber and at `AddAmmoInChamber` otherwise. From then the aim key works; a press of the trigger is refused until the `IdleStart` mark at the start of the weapon's `IDLE`. The body animator's `ProneReload` flag is up for exactly the operation's span and is raised in every movement state, not only lying; the player's movement context refuses any change of `IsInPronePose` while it is up. `ReloadFloat` lasts until `IdleStart`.

**A state's script also sets how freely the view turns.** `StateSensitivity` and `RotationSpeedClamp` on each `PlayerStateContainer`, put on the movement state's object at every entry; the clamp is degrees per frame, and 0 means no turning (the reload lying down). The sprint and its stop multiply both by `Physical.SprintSensitivity`. `probe bodystates` prints them (`turning x0.33 up to 5`).

**A jump moves by the player's clock.** Its motion is velocity times the whole-millisecond frame time, so its positions advance unevenly on even frames; on the ground the motion is the animator's and even. Recorded jumps agree with the simulation frame by frame, so this is the game's.

**The elbow's goal is a body bone.** `Bend_Goal_Left` and `Bend_Goal_Right` under the last spine bone, animated by the body's clips; `AdjustElbows` puts each on the weapon's goal by one less `First_Person_Curve_Weight * Elbow_*`. Several clips share a name across bundles with different curves (three `sprint_slow_0`, two `sprint_slow_reload`): go by what the controller's own state loads, not by the first `probe bodyclips` prints.

**A look at the weapon is its own operation.** Checking the magazine, chamber or fire mode, and examining: the sights go down at the start and the aim key and trigger are dead until `IdleStart`. Switching the fire mode is done by the idling operation and changes nothing about aiming.

**Trace numbers for animator states are CRC-32s.** The low 24 bits of the CRC-32 of the state's own name. Hashing the weapon controller's 99 names identifies any of them: 15771324 is `DRY FIRE`, 15805663 `DRY FIRE DISARMED`.

**The engine cuts a long frame** to its `Maximum Allowed Timestep`, the second number of the time manager in `globalgamemanagers`: 1/12 s here.

**Reading traces.** The game's own velocity figure alternates (3.12, 3.12, 2.78 m/s at 120 frames a second) while positions advance evenly: that is the player's clock above. The recorder's movement keys reach the game one frame late (its other commands do not); `replay` delays them to match. **Two players, two movement contexts.** `Player.CreateMovementContext` makes the plain `MovementContext`; `ClientPlayer`, the player of an online raid, overrides it with `ClientPlayerMovementContext`, which changes some rules (it refuses the prone key while `ProneReload` is up, and moves only on the server's say). **The sprint parameter starts at 1.** `MovementContext._sprintSpeed` is 1 when the character is made. Entering the sprint state sets a watch on the next change of movement state, and unless that change is to a jump the parameter is put to `StartingSprintSpeed` (0.5). So the first sprint winds up from 1 and reaches full pace in 0.90 s where a later one takes 1.43. A recording begun by a character that has not sprinted shows it in every scenario up to the first sprint.

**`Quaternion.Lerp` is not a share of the angle.** The engine blends the two quaternions' components and makes the result a unit again; for two turns about one axis `a` apart that is `2 * atan2(t * sin(a/2), 1 - t + t * cos(a/2))`. The body's turn after the view in a jump (`JumpPlayerState`) and the standing follow (`ProcessUpperbodyRotation`) use it; the walking state's smoothed yaw uses `Mathf.LerpAngle`, which is the angle's share. Read which one before writing `lerp`.

**The weapon rests in a clip named for a launcher.** The weapon animator's `Hands.IDLE` blends `akm_idle_armed` (at `LauncherID` -1) and `akm_launcher_0_idle_off` (at 0). `FirearmsAnimator.SetLauncher` sets the parameter to -1, and is only called for a weapon that has an underbarrel launcher; otherwise it stays at the controller's 0. Likewise `Hands.FIREMODE` blends two clips by `FireMode`, which is the game's `EFireMode` number (fullauto 0, single 1, doublet 2, burst 3), not the mode's place in the template's `weapFireType`. A state's clip is the tree's answer for the parameter's value; do not pick clips by name.

**The arms' solver.** One `LimbIK` (FinalIK) on each collarbone of the player prefab: bones upper arm, first forearm bone, palm; `bendModifier` 4 (toward `bendGoal`, which is `PlayerBones.BendGoals`), `bendModifierWeight` 1, `maintainRotationWeight` 0. The palm hangs from the third forearm bone, so the chain is "not direct" and the solver re-reads each bone's relation to the bending plane every update from the pose it finds. That pose is: the upper arm where `SetShoulders` put it (on the weapon rig's upper arm), everything below it as the **body's** animator has it. The game calls `solver.Update()` itself from `Player.IkApply`, then `TwistRelax.Relax()` on the second and third forearm bones of each arm (shares 0.2 and 0.4 between the first forearm bone and the palm, with two stored directions each). `PlayerBones.Shoulders` and `Upperarms` are the same two bones; `Forearms` are the first forearm bones.

**The squeeze is put on at draw time and taken off again.** `FirstPersonStrategy.ApplyFovAdjustments` turns the ribcage to the view, keeping three listed bones where they are, and scales it, the weapon's rig and the body's weapon bone (1, 1, s); `ResetFovAdjustments` undoes it. The arms are solved between, at true size. Bones recorded at draw time are squeezed about the ribcage along the view if they hang below it (the arms, the weapon's goals and markers) and not if they hang from the spine (the body's elbow goals, the body's weapon bone); a bone's recorded turning is not changed by the squeeze.

**An additive layer's curves add.** A clip on an additive layer gives a parameter its curve's value now less its value on the clip's first frame (the reference pose), times the clip's weight and the layer's, on top of what the layers below made of it. Nothing in the game's code says so; the seventh recording did. `Additive_Aiming` blends side clips in by `DeltaRotation` whose `Weapon_3rd` is 1.1 on the first frame and 1 after, so with the view 25 degrees from the body the parameter reads 1 - 0.1 * 25/45 = 0.944. When a recorded parameter is off by an amount that follows some angle or blend, look at the additive layers' clips for a curve of that name (`probe bodyclips <clip> tracks <word>` prints a clip's curves at five points).

**The hands take no input in some movement states.** `UnsuitableStates` in the settings object (bytes, like `NO_AIM_STATES`): 5 sprinting, 17 walking up to a door, 16 working one, 14 unlocking, 9 breaching, 3 crawling. The firearm's idling operation drops a press of the trigger made in one (`Player.StateIsSuitableForHandInput`); melee and a few toggles ask the same. It is asked of the movement state, not of the sprint's flag, and only by the idling operation: fire already running carries on. The trigger is a command on change (`ToggleShooting` on the press, `EndShooting` on the release), so a press that was dropped is gone.

**The weapon's line follows the clip-driven bone.** `FirearmController.WeaponOverlapping` casts from `Weapon_root` (the parent of the bone the procedural motion moves) times the position spring's value, along that parent's own barrel axis, for a length measured once from the moved bone to the muzzle or the farthest muzzle effect (0.894 m for the stock AKM). `PlayerBones.WeaponRoot` is `Weapon_root`; the bone `ShiftWeaponRoot` and the recoil move is `Weapon_Root_Anim`. So the line points where the view does, whatever the sway, the roll's pose or the body's carrying have done to the weapon. It is cast at the end of the frame (`ManualLateUpdate`, after the arms), and its depth is used by the next frame's physics steps. Before trusting a name with "root" in it, check which of the two bones it is.

**The turn-away has two poses per weapon kind and a mask.** Near (value up to 1: drawn back up to 0.4 m and canted 25 degrees) and far (value past 1: swung 57 degrees aside, hardly drawn back), blended by a figure of its own; a pose each for rifles, pistols and lying down, two more for the elbows, a curve for the camera; all `TurnAwayPose` objects beside the weapon motion on the player prefab (`probe dump` of the prefab's `ProceduralWeaponAnimation` shows the references). The effector's depth is put to zero each physics step in a crawl (mask) and while a first-person pose is held (the roll), but the controller's own depth, which the sights answer to, is not.

**The character's capsule is on the player prefab.** `CharacterControllerSpawner`: radius 0.366, height 1.6, centre 0.91 up, skin 0.1, step 0.2, slope limit 50. The spawner builds one of several controllers by a mode (the engine's own, or the game's "impostor" ones over a capsule collider). With the skin the axis stops 0.47 m from a wall, and at that distance the turned-away rifle's muzzle just clears the wall; at the bare radius it would not.

**The weapon motion's stance has a value for lying.** `MovementContext.PoseToInt`: 0 with the prone flag up, else 1 below a stance level of 0.11, else 2. Breathing and recoil index their per-stance factors by it and the turn-away picks its lying pose by it. A crouch's factors are not the lying ones.

**A host that draws faster than it simulates must show input as the simulation will take it.** The app drew the mouse's travel ahead of the frame that would apply it; where that frame scales or limits the travel (the turn rate by movement state), drawing it whole makes the view step forward and back. Anything shown ahead of a frame has to go through that frame's rules (`Movement::look_ahead`). And input gathered on the host's frames has to reach the simulation's frames by time, not by which host frame they fell in (`pacing::Frame::input_share`): at 144 drawn frames a second every sixth of the simulation's frames got two host frames' worth of mouse, which the view as shown hid and everything lagging behind the view did not. A steady view does not mean a steady simulation: test what follows the view (the body, the carried weapon), not only the view.

**In a jump the game turns the body by the player's clock too.** `JumpPlayerState` blends the body toward the view by `TRANSFORM_ROTATION_LERP_SPEED` times the player's whole-millisecond frame time, so at 120 frames a second the body turns an eighth more on every third frame. The body carries the weapon in the air (the jump's clips hand it over), so that ripple is on the weapon and arms. The simulation keeps it, as the recordings have it; what a host draws takes `Movement::drawn_body_yaw`. Whatever the game times by that clock is a candidate for the same treatment when something drawn looks uneven: check for it before looking further.

**The camera has a collider.** `PlayerOverlapManager` on the player prefab has two `ColliderExtrusion` scripts: one on `Cam` (the transform the camera's easing moves, under `CameraContainer` beside the ribcage) with a box collider, size (0.13, 0.23, 0.23), centre (0, -0.0674, -0.05) in the camera's axes; one on `playercollider` with a capsule (radius 0.22, height 0.7) about the head. `Player.LateUpdate` ends with `POM.ExtrudeCamera()`: the engine's `ComputePenetration` of the box against every collider near it on the layers `LowPolyCollider`, `DoorLowPolyCollider`, `Terrain` and `Player` (the character's own capsule left out), each answer lengthened by a millimetre, summed, and added to `Cam`'s world position. The camera's easing reads `Cam`'s local position next frame, so the push is part of its state. The rendering camera is another object that follows `Cam` in centimetre steps with the same box (`FirstPersonCameraState.CalculateSafePosition`). The recorder's `camera_pos` and `eye` are `Cam`. The capsule serves `MovementContext.LimitMotionXZ` and `RotationOverlapPrediction` (the head kept out of walls, level only): collision, not built. Before taking a gap in the eye for the camera's own motion, ask whether the box could be touching something.

**The terrain is not where the feet are.** The character counts as on the ground within its ground check and nothing pulls it down there, so it floats: 7.6 cm on the spot the eighth recording started from (its standing jump lands 7.62 cm down; the camera's box in the aimed rolls rests 7.6 to 7.7 cm below the feet). It floats lying down too. Whatever the game casts at the world or pushes out of it meets the terrain itself: the weapon's line, the camera's box. A replay that puts the ground at the feet has the camera's box touching 7.6 cm too soon and the weapon's line likewise. `ground.txt`'s third number is for this.

**The sprinting state looks at the speed limits itself.** `SprintPlayerState.ManualAnimatorMoveUpdate` calls `ProcessSpeedLimits` just before `SprintAcceleration`, besides the frame's own call in `ManualUpdate`. A limit set after the frame's look (the cap `OnJumpEnd` leaves when the animator leaves the jump) therefore holds the sprint's pace on the same frame if the state entered is the sprint, and a frame later for the walk. Only `ESpeedLimit.Fall` (the jump's cap and a fall's share it) reaches the sprint's pace at all (`StateSprintSpeedLimit`).

**A jump out of a sprint lands in the sprint.** With the keys held the movement state goes sprint, jump, sprint, with no landing state between; the sprint parameter is kept through the flight and cut by the jump's cap on landing (1.833 to 0.827 for the recorded character), then climbs back.

**A scripted turn goes through the aiming sensitivity too.** With the sights up the recorder's turn rate is multiplied by the aiming sensitivity as well as the mouse's and the movement state's factor: lying and aiming, 90 degrees asked turned the view 10. Size a script's turn from `rotation_multiplier` in a trace of the same state, or run it to the view's limit; the eighth recording's 53 and 54 stopped short of the ground for this.

**The engine's choices decide how evenly the app draws, and they were wrong for this machine.** Bevy 0.16 on Windows takes Vulkan before DirectX 12 and renders on a thread of its own. Measured with `--log-pacing` at 165 Hz on an RTX 3080: Vulkan hands the app its frames in clumps of four (18.6, 1.7, 1.0, 2.9 ms), with or without the render thread; DirectX 12 one every 6.06 ms. The render thread leaves the frame clock even (it is stamped where frames are presented) but makes one update in eight run a quarter of a frame or more early or late, and input is read at the update. The frame clock being even says nothing about when input was read: log both (`dt_ms` and `wall_ms`). And an unfocused window is updated on a 60 Hz timer, so nothing about pacing can be measured in one.

**What is drawn is a function of the host's clock.** Blending between the simulation's frames puts on screen where things were one physics step before the host's time, exactly, whatever the host's frame times (`pacing.rs`, `what_is_drawn_follows_the_hosts_time`). So unevenness on screen is unevenness of the host's clock against the moments its frames are shown, or the game's own motion; look at those two before the simulation.

**There is no falling state.** `FallDown` (a subclass of the jump's state) and the number 7 in the list of movement states exist; the factory that makes the state objects has an empty case for it, the body's controller has no state whose script names it, and its `Fall` and `Fall_End` states have no transition into them (the controller has no any-state transitions at all). `FallingDown` and `FallingDownFloat` are set by the code and read by nothing in the controller. A fall is the walking, sprinting or standing state with `ApplyGravity`: `G * FreefallTime * dt` added, the whole motion times `1 / (1 + 2 * FreefallTime)` from 0.25 s. Before building a state because a class or an animator state for it exists, check that something makes or enters it.

**The weapon's length is measured as the weapon is drawn.** `FirearmController.method_10` sets `WeaponLn` from the distance between `Weapon_root_anim` and the muzzle or farthest muzzle effect, at set-up, at `WeaponAppeared` (the draw's `WeapIn` mark or the rest's `IdleStart`) and at `WeaponModified`. In `akm_out_to_idle` the `weapon` bone is still forward of rest near the end (11.6 mm at 0.90 s of 0.967, 5.0 at 0.94, 2.5 at 0.96), and the draw hands over to rest at 0.98 of its length. So the figure depends on the frame the weapon appeared on, and can differ from one raid to the next. The ninth recording's depths fit 0.9027 m against 0.894 at rest. **A reload measures it again** (`ReloadExternalMagOperation.SwitchToIdlingState` calls `WeaponModified`): at the `MagIn` mark, or at `AddAmmoInChamber` from an empty chamber, with the weapon wherever the reload's clip has it. The tenth recording's headers: 0.8973 as first drawn, 0.9014 to 0.9043 after each of ten ordinary reloads, 0.8321 after the reload from empty. `probe clips <weapon> <clip> tracks <bone path's end> <seconds>` prints a weapon clip's tracks at a time.

**A routine the game starts takes its first turn at once.** The field of view eases in a coroutine (`CameraManager.SetFovCoroutine`), and starting a coroutine runs it to its first wait there and then: that turn sets the field of view to what it already is and counts the frame's time off. So whatever starts it, wherever in the frame, the first visible step is on the next frame. Started from the end of a frame (the obstacle's change of the sights) the simulation had it two frames on.

**The breath and the physics steps have a phase of their own.** The breath's clock runs from when the character was made; the physics clock is the engine's. Neither starts with a scenario. A replay that starts both at nothing has the weapon up to 7 mm from the game's (in step with nothing) and the camera a frame's share out through every move. Both can be read off a trace: the weapon's lift over a quiet stretch, and the frames on which a spring changed.

**The recorder's turn multiplier is the end of the frame's.** `rotation_multiplier` is written when the frame is drawn. A change of the sights made at the end of the frame (the obstacle's) is in it, though the view was turned earlier in the frame with the multiplier as it was. `replay --own-view` is one frame's turn out at each such change (0.06 and 0.31 degrees in the ninth recording), and that is the recording's doing.

**The body is clothing on a bare skeleton.** `playersuperior.bundle` has the bones and scripts; the meshes come from the customization list's bundles (`Body`, `Feet`, `Hands`, `Head`), each with a `LoddedSkin` listing a `Skin` per level of detail, fullest first, and each `Skin` naming its bones by path from `Root_Joint`. Take the first of `_lods`, not the first `Skin` found: the bundle's objects are not in that order. `probe scripts <bundle>` and `probe tree <bundle>` show a part's make-up.

**In first person the game turns the ribcage, not the collarbones.** `ApplyFovAdjustments` sets the ribcage's rotation to the hands' and its scale to (1, 1, s), after putting back the world place and turning of `PlayerBones.FovSpecialTransforms`: on the player prefab those are `Base HumanLCollarbone`, `Base HumanRCollarbone` and `Weapon_root`. It does none of it when s is 1 (field of view 50). A mesh bone tied to something that turns with the view where the game keeps it in place (or the other way about) shows as cloth stretched between the two when the view is far from the body's facing.

**An animator's root bone is not a bone like the others.** The clips move the root along the ground and the animator takes that travel off the one bone it is told is the root. The same bone asked for again, as a bone to pose, keeps the travel: a skeleton hung on it runs ahead of the character by as much again. Use the animator's own root for it (`Body::drawn_bones` does).

**One curve for every character.** `EFTHardSettings.Instance.MovementAccelerationRange` is a single object, and `PhysicalBase.OnWeightUpdated` (any character's, a bot's too, on every change of carried weight: a pick-up, a magazine out, each round fired) moves its second key to where that character's inertia puts it. The player's walking state reads it for the floor under the walk's speed when a walk begins. So a figure in the player's movement depends on who else is in the raid and what they last did. It showed only in crouched walks (which go at the floor) and looked like a dependence on bearing for two days. When a recorded figure differs from one recording to the next with everything in the header the same, look for state the game keeps outside the player: singletons, statics, anything `Instance`.

**Bones recorded as drawn say what the game adds.** The tenth recording's legs are the game's as drawn, the feet's solver and anything else done after the animator included, and they are the animator's own to a fifth of a millimetre. A solver or script that exists (`GrounderFBBIK` on the player prefab) need not be doing anything: on level ground it moves nothing. Record the bones before building what might move them.

**What moves on every frame must be drawn from every frame.** The weapon's springs move on physics steps, so what is drawn of them is blended between frames a physics step apart. The body's animation moves on every frame, and blended the same way a fast limb is drawn off its path on the frames between (25 mm for a sprinting foot), on and off sixty times a second. A foot's path *in the simulation* was even, and was measured so, while the path *as drawn* was not: measure what is drawn (`--log-pacing` writes the drawn feet, pelvis and ribcage) when the complaint is about what is seen.

**The upper body faces the view; the legs need not.** The body's animation turns the spine with the view's yaw over standing legs, so the ribcage (and the eye hung on it) goes round the feet with the view even while the body's own facing stands still, and bends far forward with the view's pitch: looking straight down, the eye is half a metre ahead of the feet (recorded crouched, 0.495 m). A leg is seen from there only as it swings forward. Keep the ribcage's place in the view's frame when drawing ahead of the simulation; in the world's frame it trails the view.

**An additive layer's clips are blended before they are added.** Each clip's difference from its own reference pose (a shift and a turn per bone) is blended with the other clips' differences like the clips of any blend tree, and the result is added to the pose once. Adding them one after another, each by its own weight, is the same for one clip and for turns about one axis and parts wherever the clips turn a bone about different axes: the layer that bends the body with the view, when the view is pitched and turned from the body at once. Nothing in the game's code says which; the recordings did (the ribcage 3 to 5.5 mm out at 9.5 degrees of pitch, exact when blended first). When a gap grows with the product of two angles, look at how two rotations are being combined.

**The eye trails the view by one frame, and a host must add nothing to that.** The body's animation poses the upper body for the angles noted before the frame's own turn of the view, so the ribcage (the eye is on it) goes round the feet, and forward and down with the pitch, one frame behind the view and is at rest one frame after it. With the view far down the ribcage is 0.44 m ahead of the feet and a frame's turn at 300 degrees a second is 2 cm. A host that shows the view ahead of the simulation and the body from frames some way behind has to draw the ribcage for the view it shows (`Movement::ribcage_posed_for`): drawn from the old frames, the eye and everything under it kept moving for 35 ms after the mouse had stopped. A thing that follows the view in the game with a lag of its own must be drawn with that lag measured from the view as shown, not carried in old frames.

**A view from outside has no squeeze and none of the first-person hands.** The squeeze is `FirstPersonStrategy`'s; `ThirdPersonStrategy.ApplyFovAdjustments` sets the scale to 1. `PlayerBody.UpdatePlayerRenders` turns the hands' renderers and the watch off from outside; the upper body's skin has sleeves and hands of its own.

**A shot's kick is given late in the frame, and a single shot is two frames' work.** `InitiateShot` only raises a flag; `FirearmController.ManualLateUpdate`, called at the end of `Player.VisualPass` after the springs have had the frame's physics steps, calls the recoil's `Shoot`. So the springs take a shot on the first physics step of a later frame. The single-shot operation takes its round in `Start` (`PrepareShot`: the count drops on the frame of the press) and makes the shot in `OnFireEvent`, when the fire state's `FiringBullet` mark reaches it on the animator's next update: a frame later. The automatic operation does both in the mark's call. Recorded in when the springs first move after the first shot of each firing scenario. A count that drops is not the shot; ask which call a thing is made in before timing something else by it.

**After a quarter turn the feet stand past the view.** The turn on the spot swings the body 90 degrees from 45 behind the view, so it ends up to 45 ahead of it (recorded: 140 and 185). By the animator's conditions a turn of the view back past what is left of the 45 starts the quarter turn the other way, and one begun while the other still plays is blended into over a tenth of the state (`Turn_Left_90_Aim` to `Turn_Right_90_Aim`, open to being cut short by the transitions above it). None of that is recorded with a mouse's kind of input yet (scenarios 58 to 60).

**The game takes the speed wheel a notch every tenth of a second.** A recording can carry input nobody scripted (the tenth's 17: seven notches down, 0.100 to 0.109 s apart). The recorder's own wheel presses go straight to the player and are not held to that pace. Before taking a gap in one scenario for a rule, look at whether the game's own inputs (`speed`, `pose`, `move`) changed when the script did not ask.

**The angle from body to view is rounded, and exactly 45 is not past 45.** `MovementContext.ApplyRotation` takes it as `Mathf.DeltaAngle(360f + body, 360f + view)`: single precision past 512 comes in steps of 1/16384 of a degree, so the angle does too. The body's own rotation is a quaternion, far finer, so the body's following of the view runs on until the angle *reads* the limit exactly, and the animator's condition for a turn on the spot is greater-than. A simulation that keeps the body as a bearing in single precision stalls a step or two short (45.000015) and turns again. Where a comparison against a threshold decides something, look at how the game arrives at the number, to the last bit, and what holds the state it is read from.

**The sway sees the view a frame late.** The motion effector's tracking transform is the weapon rig's root. `Player.VisualPass` calls `ProcessEffectors` (which works the frame's physics steps) and only then `MouseLook` (which turns the rig to the view). So the gyro goes by the view of the frame before. Before taking a transform a script reads as current, find where in the frame it is written and where it is read.

**"Within 0.13 degrees" was a frame's offset.** The sway had agreed with the game "within 0.13 degrees" since the second recording. Slow scripted turns cannot tell a frame's lead from noise; flicks at 420 degrees a second made it 0.83 degrees and unmistakable, and with the cause found the gap is 0.0000 in the slow turns too. A small steady gap that scales with how fast something moves is a timing offset: record the fast case.

**The weapon's motion goes by the weight the weapon had when last taken stock of.** `ProceduralWeaponAnimation.UpdateWeaponVariables` (the ergonomic weight, the sway's and displacement's strengths, the aiming speed, the aim sway) runs when the weapon is drawn, modified, or a reload ends, and not when a round is fired. A trace's header gives both `weapon_weight` (now) and `ergonomic_weight` (as last taken); late in a magazine they disagree by the rounds fired.

**The engine's animator runs after every script's update.** The body's animator is the engine's own here, so its state callbacks (a movement state entered, the jump's charge, the sights put down by a state) come after `Player.UpdateTick` in the frame, and `Physical.Update` (stamina) is in `UpdateTick`. What a state entered in a frame sets running is first paid for in the next frame; what it charges comes off at once. The recorder writes at draw time, after both: a flag that is down in a frame's record may still have been up when that frame's update ran.

**Hunger and thirst move the numbers during a recording.** Stamina's refill is times a factor of how well fed the character is (0.917 at full, 1 from 90% down to 10%), and every drain times a factor of how well watered (1 down to 90%, rising below). Both fall all through a raid: over eleven minutes the legs' refill went from 5.90 to 6.30 a second and a sprint's cost from 4.000 to 4.087. A rate that drifts smoothly through a recording, with everything in the header the same, is the health system: look at `HealthController` before the formula.

**The stance's tally is never cleared.** `MovementContext._accumulatedDeltaPose` adds up every rise of the smoothed stance, charges a third of a notch whenever three notches' worth is in it, and takes only a third of a notch off. So the first 0.44 of rise in a character's life is free, and from then on every 0.055 of rise costs. Where a recording starts, the tally holds whatever the raid so far left in it; every charge in a scenario bounds it and the slow end of a rise bounds it closest.

**Misnamed properties.** In the decompiled `ProceduralWeaponAnimation`, `HeadBobbing` returns the field-of-view setting and `FarAimPlane` returns the head-bobbing setting; `FieldOfView` is a blind-fire flag. Follow what a property returns, not its name.

**A setting can lead to code that never runs.** The game has an "automatic vaulting" setting and a whole second set of limits for it in `globals.json`. The path it opens needs two switches (`IsEnableAutoVault`, `IsEnableAutoClimb`) that start false and that only the game's vaulting debug tool sets. Like the sprint zoom and the falling state: before building what a setting or a class promises, find the line that turns it on.

**The jump is on the key's release.** `Control.ini` in the install binds `Jump` and `Vaulting` both to Space, `Jump` with press type `Release` and `Vaulting` with `Continuous`. The key bindings carry a press type as well as a key; a host that copies the keys has to copy that too. `Player.Jump` then refuses when the vault key's timer has passed 0.6 s.

**A vault's root barely leaves the ground.** `Jump_over_100` moves its root 1.65 m forward and no higher than 15 cm, and the clip keeps even that in the pose; the body goes over the wall in the pose alone while the game shrinks the capsule from below so that it passes over the top. A climb's clips do carry the rise as root motion. Look at a clip's motion curves (`probe bodyclips <clip> tracks Root_Joint`) before deciding how a move gets the character where it ends.

**A climb ends short of the edge.** The clip's forward travel is partly lost against the obstacle's face while the capsule is still low, and the way out of the state blends the rest away: from against a box a metre high the character's axis ends about 10 cm short of the top's edge, at the top's height. It stays up there by the capsule's rounded foot resting on the edge, which is the engine's collision. A stand-in world with a flat-footed character drops it back down: the first climb in the simulation's test did exactly that.

**The grid measures from ahead of the feet.** `VaultingGridRoot` is a child of the animated `Player` object at the feet, facing as the body faces, and each measurement moves it forward by a tenth of the character's forward speed for the next. The obstacle's "distance" is from that root, so a sprinting character can be 0.9 m from a wall and within the 0.5 m a vault allows. The root is moved between the rays and the obstacle's sums within one measurement, so with a changing speed the points and the drop behind are placed by two different roots; here both are kept as the game has them.

**A kept figure is as old as its last reason to change.** `Physical`'s per-second costs are cached (`Deferred`) and each is marked stale by a list of events written by hand. Aiming's cost has hydration in it and hydration is not on its list. It showed as 0.005 of 80 in one scenario, only because the recorder now writes hydration and a step of it happened to fall inside a spell of aiming. When a figure the game works out from several inputs is cached, read what invalidates the cache, not only the formula.

**A reload's end takes stock of the weapon.** `ReloadExternalMagOperation.SwitchToIdlingState` calls `WeaponModified`, which measures the weapon's line again (tenth recording) and, through `RecalculateErgonomic`, works the ergonomic weight out again and calls `Physical.Aim` with it (twelfth recording, scenario 48: the arms drain 0.16% faster from the reload's end, for the one round more in the new magazine). The length is in the replay since 2026-10-10; the weight is not.

**A distance in the world is not a length of the weapon while the squeeze is on.** `WeaponLn` is `Vector3.Distance` of two world positions, taken at a point in the frame where the first-person squeeze of the frame before's drawing is still on the rig, and then divided by the muzzle's `lossyScale.y`. The spec had read that division as cancelling the squeeze and moved on; it cancels it only along the barrel's own axis. Measured plainly, the reload from empty was 27 mm from the game's figure, and every explanation tried first (which frame's pose, which muzzle effect) moved it by a millimetre or two. Two things to keep from it: when the game's code corrects for something (here a division by a scale), work out what the correction is exact for, because the recorded figure is the inexact one; and `lossyScale` of a bone turned against a squeeze is how far its own axis, squeezed, still reaches along itself (`1 - (1 - s) * c * c` for a share `c` along the view), not the squeeze.

**Which state classes inherit a key.** Whether a key does anything in a movement state is a question of which class the state's object is and what that class inherits: `IdlePlayerState.Vaulting` tries a vault, and the sidestep, going down and getting up are all built on `IdlePlayerState` and do not take it back (lying still and the roll do). A try made there cannot be taken by the animator, but leaves its flag up for when it can. Grep for `: IdlePlayerState` and `: MovePlayerState` before listing the states a key works in.

**Input.** `EFT\MoveInputTranslator.cs` maps commands to player calls. Sprint is a toggle command on key press that the idle state ignores, plus an explicit stop on release. The body's turn limit behind the view is `HANDS_TO_BODY_MAX_ANGLE` from the settings object.

**Settings object.** `EFTHardSettings` is the object `eftsettings` in the loose file `resources.assets`, which has no type trees. It is read with a field layout generated from the class definition (`crates\trp-data\src\hard_settings_layout.txt`). Several values differ from the defaults written in the code, so the object must be read, not assumed. `probe hardsettings [filter]` prints it.

## Added 2026-10-10, evening (the thirteenth recording and the vault traces)

**The player's controller is the game's own.** `AppEnvironment.Config.CharacterController.ClientPlayerMode` is `ControllerType.Simple`, so `CharacterControllerSpawner` builds `SimpleCharacterController` (a thousand lines of readable code) over a capsule collider, and the prefab's skin never reaches it. A figure on a prefab says nothing until you know which of the classes that could read it is the one made: find the `new`. The recorded capsule (centre 0.8 for a height of 1.6) gave it away.

**A capsule put back to full size inside something is pushed out of it, slowly.** The controller cuts any step of 0.2 m or more to 0.05 m, and the state's cap on speed cuts that again. After a vault the character leaves the wall at exactly the cap for nine frames. A run of frames at exactly a cap is something being pushed, not something moving.

**With the upward push off, the ground eats moves.** In a vault or a climb the controller drops the upward part of every way out; on rising ground the capsule then sinks a millimetre a frame until a check meant for an engine bug finds it sunk and throws the frame's move away. One frame in three at the start of a recorded climb has no travel.

**The game stops a character that pushes at a wall.** `ObstacleCollision`: a lock on walking and sprinting, by the character's real speed, which is why a sprint into a wall ends in the sprint's stop and a walk into one runs out into standing. It is separate from the controller, which only keeps the capsule out.

**A transition cut short is held as the frame before left it.** Not dropped, and not as the frame of the cut would have had it: a curve only the new states carry reads on the frame of the cut what it read the frame before. The held blend keeps the root moving at the pace it last had. The engine goes on reporting the old state as current, with its clock a frame further on and then still. To learn what the engine does in a blend, find a curve that is 1 in one state and absent in the other: the recorded parameter is then the weight itself (`FootStep` in the walk).

**The length of a blend out of a held blend is not a share of what it leaves.** Twice it came to the share over 1.25 (the held states' pace) of the state being *entered*. Two cases with the same share and the same pace: provisional.

**A recording keeps a path, not a map.** `pos.y - ground_below` gives the ground under the character's axis wherever it went, which is enough to walk the same line again, with a wall as a step in it. It cannot hold a wall that is askew to the line (the character slides along it) or anything run round. Stand square to an obstacle when recording at one.

**A wall rebuilt from a trace is placed to the distance between two frames.** The step shows between the last frame over low ground and the first over high: 2 to 4 cm at a walk. What follows inherits it (a fall a frame early). When two runs differ by one frame from some point on, look for a place a centimetre or two out before looking for a rule.

**A press of the recorder's jump key that comes too early is not always lost.** In `61-legs-dry` presses 1.5 s apart met jumps 1.53 s long: each was taken a little later than pressed, until one fell too early and did nothing. A script that counts on an action must leave a margin or wait on a condition.

**The crouch key works in the air,** and a class that does not override a handler has the base class's, which usually does the plain thing. The simulation had refused the stance change in a jump because the vault refuses it. List what a state's class overrides before deciding what it ignores (`grep -n 'override' <State>.cs`).

## Added 2026-10-11, the session after the evening's (the kind of thing in hand, the weapon's animator, the lean)

**The weapon's animator.**
- **It is the engine's own, updated after every script, like the body's.** The local player's arms are made with the update mode `Auto` (`BaseLocalGame`), and only `UseHandsFastAnimator` in a config this install lacks makes it the game's own "fast" animator stepped by hand. So `Player.ArmsUpdate` runs the controller's operation and nothing else, the engine then updates the animator, and the marks are delivered from `ArmsLateUpdate`, which `ComplexLateUpdate` calls at the very end of `Player.LateUpdate`, after `VisualPass` and so after the firearm controller's own late update has given the recoil its shot.
- **That order is the frame between a single shot's kick and an automatic one's.** A single shot is made by a mark's handler (the end of frame N), so the recoil gets it in frame N+1's late update and the springs feel it from N+2; automatic fire's first shot is made by the command (frame N's update) and the springs feel it from N+1. It had been recorded as "2 or 3 frames, never 1" against "1 or 2" and built as a one-step delay; the reason is this.
- **A mark fires when the state is next looked at, not at its time.** Each state keeps the point it had reached; a mark fires if it lies at or after that point and before the state's time now. Three things follow that the fixed sequence did not have: a state being left fires *all* its remaining marks on the first update of the transition out (`MagHide` at 0.779 of `RELOAD OUT`, not 0.850); a mark at exactly 1 (`WeapIn` in the draw) fires only as the state is left, so `IdleStart` comes first and it is `IdleStart` that tells the game the weapon has appeared (25 ms before `WeapIn` for the AKM); and a state entered at exactly 0 fires nothing until it has moved.
- **Marks carry conditions, tested when delivered.** Kinds: 0 a whole number, 1 a float, 2 a flag; modes: 0 equal, 1 not equal, 2 greater, 3 less, 4 at least, 5 at most. A float has tests only for greater and less; a float condition in any other mode would throw in the game. The reader's own comment had the first two kinds the wrong way round.
- **An exit point is a crossing, every cycle, loop or not.** `DRY FIRE` does not loop, and its way out "at 1 once the trigger is up" is taken at 2 when the trigger came up at 1.44. The game's own animator code tests exactly that (`AnimatorControllerTransition.IsTimeForTransition`). The engine here had "passed once, passed for good" for a state that does not loop; the body's fourteen recordings replay the same line for line either way, so nothing in them had ever asked.
- **The game asks the animator for a state outright in three places that are built:** `FIRE` at 0.2 (a single shot, and automatic fire's start), and `IDLE` at 0.1 when automatic fire hands back to idling. Everything else is parameters and the animator's own transitions: a reload is one trigger (`Reload`) and three flags, and `FIRE` is entered again between automatic shots by `IDLE`'s own way to it while `Fire` is up.

## Added 2026-10-12 (the first frames, the fire-mode switch, the hands on the world)

- **Why a thing fails to draw the first time it is seen.** Bevy builds a render pipeline (shaders compiled for one mesh layout and one sort of material) the first time something needing it is queued for a view, on its background task pool, and skips the thing until the pipeline is ready. On DirectX 12 each opaque mesh pipeline takes over a second (four build side by side; a fifth waits for a free thread and adds a second). A hidden entity is never queued, so its pipelines are not asked for until it is shown: the app's weapon, hidden for a bare start, was missing again at its first draw. `synchronous_pipeline_compilation` cures the missing but builds one after another: 3.6 s and two more of a second for the first three frames. What works: have everything in view behind a curtain and wait for `PipelineCache::waiting_pipelines()` to stay empty (read in the render world, handed over in an atomic). Vulkan builds the same set in 0.45 s against 1.8 s.
