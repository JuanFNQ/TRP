### How it is stepped, as it stood

**How it is stepped.** The simulation runs on a frame clock of its own, 120 frames a second whatever the rate of drawing (`trp_sim::Pacer`; `--frame-rate` changes it). Each of its frames is one frame of the game: the player is moved and the weapon's logic run once, and the weapon's springs are stepped once for each 1/60 s physics step that falls in it. The reason is that the game's own results change with its frame rate (its aiming camera is half as fast again at 120 frames a second as at 60, and uneven; a stop runs down twice as fast; shots and reload stages land on frame boundaries), and a runtime laid over another game must not take that from the host's frame rate. 120 is the rate every recording was made and compared at, so this is the game as measured. What is drawn is the simulation as it stood one physics step (17 ms) before the host's time, which is smooth at any rate of drawing: what the weapon's springs move is blended between the last two frames that had a physics step, since the springs move only there, and what moves on every frame (the player's place, the ribcage the view hangs from, the body's skeleton) between the two frames either side of that moment; the view's own turning is drawn at once, with the mouse's travel that no frame has had yet shown as the next frame will let it through. That travel goes to the frames by their own time, not by which drawn frame they fell in. Two things the game does unevenly on even frames, because it times the player by a clock in whole milliseconds, are evened out in what is drawn and nowhere else: a jump's travel through the air and the body's turn after the view in a jump. The game itself does not blend: at 120 frames a second its weapon springs change every other frame. What is drawn follows the host's clock exactly, a physics step behind, however unevenly the host's frames come (`cargo test -p trp-sim --lib pacing -- --nocapture`); so an app whose frames reach the screen at other moments than its clock says draws unevenly whatever the simulation does, which is why the backend and the render thread above matter. With `--hold`, the scripted presses come at set seconds (aim at a half, sprint and lying down at one, a jump, a reload and automatic fire at two), while `--screenshot-frame` counts drawn frames, so pick a late frame.

### Comparing with the game: the replay and the recorder version by version, as it stood

The first line of each game trace records what the game had: weapon weight, ergonomics, overweight, inertia, the top-speed setting, field of view and head bobbing. `replay` takes over the top speed, the two view settings and the rounds loaded from the trace, so the two start alike. Without `--weight` it finds, for each scenario, the carried weight that gives that scenario's recorded inertia (the weight changes within a recording: a reload that drops a magazine lightens the character). Recoil and breathing draw random numbers, so they compare within the game's own spread and in size and tempo, not moment by moment.

Where a trace exists the replay follows it frame by frame: the same frame times, the same look direction on the same compass bearing, and the weapon weight, round and aiming speed it shows. It also starts the two things that run on the game's own clocks where the recording has them: the breath (a loop of a few seconds that lifts the weapon up to 7 mm; fitted to the weapon's rise and fall over the scenario's quiet first half second) and the physics steps (the game's springs move on every other frame at 120 frames a second, and which frames those are shows in the trace). Scripted turn rates pass through the game's mouse sensitivity, so the recorded look direction is used rather than the script's.

The game times the player by a stopwatch in whole milliseconds, apart from the engine's frame time, and the replay steps the movement states by the one and the animator by the other. The recorder writes both from version 0.2.0 on (`clock_dt` beside `dt`). An older trace has only the engine's; there the player's is worked out from the game's velocity figure, which is the frame's motion over it, on every frame in which the player moved.

0.4.4 (the tenth recording was made with it, 2026-10-09) is 0.4.3 with the lower body added: on every frame the pelvis, the first spine bone and each leg down to the toe as drawn, in the player's axes (`pelvis_p`, `left_thigh1_q`, `right_foot_p` and so on), and in the header how each part of the body is drawn for the player (`point_of_view`, and `skin_Body`, `skin_Feet`, `skin_Hands`, `skin_Head`: renderers on or off, and how they cast shadows). The replay poses the same bones and writes them under the same names.

0.4.3 (built 2026-10-09, never run by itself: 0.4.4 has all of it) has scenario 53 put right (its second turn down now reaches the view's limit, so the aim key is let go and pressed with the weapon pushed back past the point where the sights drop; the ninth recording's second pass stopped 8 cm in, short of it) and writes two things the ninth recording had to be fitted for: in the header the length of the line the game casts along the weapon (`weapon_length`, the game's own figure), and on every frame how far under the feet the ground is (`ground_below`, a ray down from the character on the layers the weapon's line is cast on). The replay takes both where a trace has them. For a recording without, the line's length can be given in a `weapon_length.txt` in the traces' folder or with `replay --line <metres>`. With `ground_below` a walk off a ledge records the ledge's own profile (`hosts\spt-oracle\scenarios-ledge\50-fall-from-ledge.json`, run alone, 3 m back from a ledge and facing it); the replay does not rebuild ground from it yet.

A recording's `ground.txt` may carry a third number: how far over the ground the character stood on the spot the scenarios start from. The game's character rides up to 8 cm over the ground, and what the game casts into the world or pushes out of it (the weapon's line, the camera's box) meets the ground itself. A standing jump from the spot lands that much lower (scenario 08's last frame: 0.0762 m in the eighth recording). The replay puts the terrain there for a scenario that has no jump of its own to find it by and whose character had not fallen onto the ground before it began.

0.4.0 (the seventh recording was made with it) is for what the sixth recording does not have: a reload and a magazine check in a sprint, the view turned in a sprint, a jump and a crawl, the prone key in a reload, a crawl into a reload, and the aim key and trigger just after the magazine goes in (scenarios 41 to 49). It takes two more presses in a script (`check_magazine`, `check_chamber`) and writes, per frame, what the script asked the view to turn and what the movement state allows of it (`turn_asked`, `rotation_multiplier`, `turn_factor`, `turn_limit`), which operation the weapon's logic has in hand (`operation`), and the points the elbows bend toward, the body's and the weapon's (`left_bend_goal`, `weapon_left_bend_goal` and the two for the right). It needs four spare loaded magazines within reach.

What the replay does with those: it sets its own turn factor and limit beside the game's and its own operation in hand beside the recorded one (`in_hand`: a reload, a look at the weapon, or neither). `replay --own-view` turns the view from what the script asked, through the simulation's own limits, where otherwise it follows the view the game recorded; the view it ends with is then compared like any other value (`look`).

The replay also holds the weapon. It keeps the weapon's rig with no engine (`crates/trp-sim/src/rig.rs`), plays the weapon's clips on it as the weapon's logic asks, and solves both arms onto it each frame with the simulation's solver (`crates/trp-sim/src/arms.rs`). Beside a recording that has them (0.3.0 on) it sets each arm's upper arm, forearm and palm, the two hand markers and the weapon's own bone as its clips have them, and the elbows' goals. With 0.4.0's goals it also runs the solver on the game's own palm and elbow goal (`..._given_q`, `..._given_p`), which shows the solver apart from where the weapon is. Where a recording has the weapon's own bone and right-hand marker, the sight and the hand's hold on the weapon are taken from those.

The replay's ground is even, and level unless the traces' folder holds a `ground.txt`: two numbers, the rise in metres per metre along the world's x and z (lines starting with `#` are notes). The fourth, fifth and sixth recordings were made on the same patch, which rises 3.8 mm a metre toward -z (the sixth's own `surface_normal` says so; for the other two it was fitted from how the walks climbed); with that in, a lying body rolls as the game's did and the eye in the walks agrees within a millimetre.

The recorder's movement keys reach the game one frame after the script's time, and the replay delays them the same way; its other commands are on time.

The recordings are made on a real map, so the ground is not always level and not always clear. In the fourth the patch sloped 0.4% and had a dip and a kerb 18 m out, which a sprint crossed; only the eye's height shows it. In the third recording, made before the recorder put the player back between scenarios, the player walked into something in scenarios 14 and 17, jumped onto ground 1.8 m higher in 09, and ran over a slope in 07 and 12, so those distances or heights do not compare.
